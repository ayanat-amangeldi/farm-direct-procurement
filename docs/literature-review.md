## 3.1. Scope and Approach

This review examines research relevant to developing an information system for direct procurement of agricultural products from small-scale farms with transparent pricing. It addresses four connected topics: short food supply chains, digital agricultural marketplaces, information transparency and trust, and the adoption of digital tools by farmers and buyers.

The review includes ten peer-reviewed journal articles and two institutional publications from FAO and OECD, published between 2022 and 2025. Sources were selected for their relevance to procurement processes, user participation, information exchange, and platform design. The review follows a targeted narrative approach rather than an exhaustive systematic-review protocol.

The analysis distinguishes between reported research findings and their interpretation for this project. Evidence from other countries informs the design but does not establish that the same outcomes will occur in Kazakhstan.

## 3.2. Short Food Supply Chains and Procurement Coordination

Short food supply chains provide alternatives to conventional distribution arrangements by strengthening connections between producers and consumers. However, reducing the number of intermediaries does not automatically resolve organisational and operational difficulties. Bayir et al. identify challenges across supply-chain processes and show that order management, sourcing, and other operational activities receive less detailed attention than general production and distribution issues. This supports examining the complete procurement workflow rather than treating direct sales as a catalog alone. [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/su14053029)

Cirone et al. approach the problem through coordination between producer capabilities and consumer expectations. Their mixed-method study combines household research, stakeholder discussions, and expert consultation to develop business strategies for an Italian short food supply chain. Its contribution is the joint consideration of supply and demand. However, findings from a specific regional grain supply chain cannot directly determine requirements for all products or purchasing contexts. [2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spc.2023.07.017)

For the proposed system, these studies motivate connected functions for offer publication, quantity selection, order confirmation, and fulfilment. This is a project design interpretation: the literature supports coordinated processes but does not prescribe the exact screens or order states.

## 3.3. Information Availability, Trust, and Buyer Decisions

Herzig and Zander’s review of 30 empirical studies identifies information availability, trust, and contextual factors such as accessibility and convenience as important influences on consumer behaviour. Their findings indicate that purchasing decisions cannot be explained through price or demographic characteristics alone. However, the review concerns European Union consumers, which limits its direct transfer to Kazakhstan. [3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40100-025-00370-w)

Sadeli et al. investigate agricultural purchasing through social commerce in Indonesia. Their model connects information seeking, product knowledge, perceived sustainability, attitudes, and purchasing intentions. The study supports providing meaningful information alongside offers. Nevertheless, associations identified through survey modelling should not be interpreted as proof that adding information to an application will cause higher sales. [4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2022.135812)

A more cautious perspective appears in Charatsari et al. Their survey of 211 Greek consumers identifies scepticism about the effects of agricultural digitalisation, particularly its social implications. The study demonstrates that acceptance should be investigated rather than assumed. Its scope includes broader agricultural technologies, so the results cannot be treated as a direct evaluation of a simple procurement application. [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/su162411241)

Together, these sources support displaying farmer identity, product information, purchasing conditions, and understandable prices. They also motivate evaluating whether users understand and trust the information presented.

## 3.4. Farmer Adoption and Platform Governance

Maina et al. examine participation in the M-shamba digital marketing platform using data from 375 potato farmers in Kenya. Their analysis distinguishes platform use from the proportion of production actually marketed through it. Participation is associated with several economic and household characteristics. This distinction matters because registration or access alone does not demonstrate sustained practical use. The observational, region-specific findings do not establish the effects of the proposed application. [6](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsufs.2024.1307978)

Masi et al. investigate anticipated adoption of two digital solutions among 31 farmers participating in Italian short food supply chains. Farmers showed greater willingness to adopt the simpler commercialisation solution. This supports prioritising understandable tasks and limited interaction complexity. However, anticipated adoption is different from observed long-term use, and the small sample limits generalisation. [7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40100-025-00377-3)

Klingenberg et al. distinguish value creation from value capture in agricultural digitalisation. Their analysis indicates that platforms can create value while the benefits received by farmers depend on competitive dynamics and governance. Consequently, making prices visible should not be equated with guaranteeing fair prices or higher farmer income. [8](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agsy.2022.103468)

For this project, transparency therefore means making recorded purchasing conditions understandable and traceable. It does not imply that the system determines an objectively fair market price.

## 3.5. Operational Visibility and Inclusive Design

Ušča and Tisenkopfs examine a direct purchasing network in Latvia during the COVID-19 pandemic. Their study connects adaptation with organisational changes, participant relationships, and innovations in the network. Suali et al., studying UK food platforms, similarly highlight information flows and operational flexibility. These studies support considering coordination and visibility alongside marketplace functions. However, pandemic conditions and established distribution networks differ from the operating conditions of an early local prototype. [9](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsufs.2023.1146446), [10](https://doi.org/10.1108/SCM-02-2023-0064)

Institutional evidence adds an inclusion perspective. FAO identifies digital literacy, infrastructure, and financial constraints as barriers affecting small-scale producers. OECD highlights costs, usability, skills, relevance, and trust among barriers to agricultural digitalisation. These publications cover technologies beyond procurement platforms, but their findings motivate simple workflows and evaluation with users who have different levels of digital experience. [11](https://doi.org/10.4060/cb9479en), [12](https://doi.org/10.1787/285cc27d-en)

## 3.7. Synthesis and Implications for the Project

The reviewed literature supports three connected design priorities: comparable purchasing information, coordinated procurement processes, and accessible interaction. It also shows why digital access, visible prices, and shorter distribution arrangements should not be treated as sufficient evidence of improved outcomes.

Based on this synthesis, the project adopts the following design decisions:

1. Comparable offers. Separate product types from farmer offers and present consistent fields for unit price, available quantity, minimum order, and fulfilment conditions.

2. Explicit total cost. Show the product subtotal, delivery charge, and final amount before order submission.

3. Preserved purchasing terms. Store the price and quantity accepted when an order is placed so that later offer changes do not alter existing agreements.

4. Traceable changes. Record price updates and order transitions with timestamps.

5. Role-specific workflows. Provide focused buyer, farmer, and administrator functions.

6. Task-based evaluation. Assess whether intended users can compare offers, understand totals, manage offers, and follow order progress.

These decisions are engineering interpretations of the reviewed evidence and the project interviews. In particular, price history and preservation of accepted terms are proposed mechanisms whose usefulness must be evaluated.

The project’s contribution is an integrated implementation and evaluation of these procurement functions in the intended small-farm context. The current prototype demonstrates local workflows. Improvements in income, transaction costs, trust, or operational efficiency remain outcomes to investigate through subsequent evaluation.
